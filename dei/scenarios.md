## Scenario 1: PYP - Nickname  

Context: Peers keep calling a student a nickname they've asked them to stop using. 

Teacher: (to student #1, individually) "What's going on? And on a 1–10, how ready are you to talk this out with them?" 
 STUDENT #1: "A 6. I just want them to stop." 
 TEACHER: (to student #2, individually) "What's the nickname, and what does it mean?" (If it's in another language, ask plainly: "Translate it for me.") 
 STUDENT #2:"It's just a joke, it doesn't mean anything bad." 
 TEACHER: (with both, to student #1) "Go ahead." 
 STUDENT #1:"I feel disrespected. I've asked you to stop and you keep doing it, and it's worse because I don't even know exactly what it means." 
 TEACHER: (to student #2) "What did you hear?" 
 STUDENT #2:"That she doesn't like the name." 
 TEACHER: "It’s more than that. I heard she felt disrespected because she asked you to stop, and it kept going. Say that back." 
 STUDENT #2:"You feel disrespected because you asked me to stop and I didn't." 
 TEACHER: (to student #1) "Is that correct?" 
 STUDENT #1:"Yeah." 
 TEACHER: (to student #2) "Your turn." 
 STUDENT #2:"I feel like it started as a friend thing, I didn't think it was a big deal." 
 TEACHER: "Here's the simple rule, though: when someone asks you to stop calling them a name, that's the moment you stop, joke or not" 
 STUDENT #2:"Okay. I'll stop it. My bad." 

TEACHER: What's different going forward?" 
STUDENT #2:"I use her actual name." 

TEACHER: “Anything you want to say before we leave this?” 

STUDENT #2: “I’m sorry” 
 

(Debrief note: the "when they ask you to stop, you stop" line is the whole lesson here.)  

Group questions:
1. Can you lean on school policy to support your discussion about nicknames?
2. Are there signs that a student may not like a nickname others have given them, even if they respond to it?
3. What would you do differently or add to this scenario?

## Scenario 2: PYP - Excluded  

Context : A student comes in upset because a group of friends wouldn't let them play. The group says it's "not their fault." 

TEACHER: (to student #1, individually) "You seem really upset, tell me what zone you're in right now? We can wait until you're ready to talk about it."
 STUDENT #1: "I'm in the blue zone, I can talk about it."
 STUDENT #1: "They said they didn't want to play with me."
 TEACHER: (brings student #1 and one or two of the group, not a crowd. To student #1) "Tell them how that felt."
 STUDENT #1: "I felt left out. You're my friend and you told me to go away in front of everyone."
 TEACHER: (to Student #2) "What did you hear?"
 STUDENT #2: "I mean, it wasn't just me, everyone said..."
 TEACHER: "I'm going to pause you there. I didn't ask whose fault it is. I asked what you heard them say."
 STUDENT #2: "That she felt left out and embarrassed."
 TEACHER: (to student #1) "Yes. Did you feel heard?"
 STUDENT #1: "Yeah."
 TEACHER: (to student #2) "Now your side. Explain using 'I felt,' not 'we didn't.'"
 STUDENT #2: "I feel like we already had teams and it got awkward, and then it turned into a thing."
 TEACHER: (to student #1) "What'd you hear?"
 STUDENT #1: "That the game was already set up and it got weird, not that you hate me."
 TEACHER: "So going forward, what do you do when the game's already going and someone wants in?"
 STUDENT #2: "We can say 'next round' instead of 'go away.'"
 TEACHER: "Anything to close this out?"
 STUDENT #2: "You can play with us tomorrow. Sorry it came out mean." 

(Debrief note: watch how the mediator refuses to chase "whose fault" and keeps pulling back to impact.) 

Group questions:
1. Why do we think the teacher avoided discussing blame?
2. How might teachers build an inclusive environment while balancing student social dynamics?
3. Does this approach feel realistic for your grade level?

## Scenario 3: PYP - "Don't Play With Them" 

Context : A student overheard a peer telling others not to play with them. 

TEACHER: (to student #1, individually) "I saw your face. What did you hear? And on a 1–10, how ready are you to talk about it?" 
 STUDENT #1:"A 4. It really stung." 
 TEACHER: "That's okay. Let's give it a little time. I'll grab you in a bit." (Later, Student #1 is at a 6.) 
 TEACHER: (both at table, to student #1) "Tell them what that was like." 
 STUDENT #1:"I heard you tell everyone not to play with me. I felt like nobody wanted me around." 
 TEACHER: (to student #2) "What did you hear?" 
 STUDENT #2: "That she's upset I said that." 
 TEACHER: "It’s deeper than that. I heard her say she felt like nobody wanted her. Say that back." 
 STUDENT #2: "You felt like no one wanted you there." 
 TEACHER: (to student #1) "Does that sound right to you?" 
 STUDENT #1:"Yeah." 
 TEACHER: (Use a Phoenix apology if it feels appropriate) "Now you can explain your side." 
 STUDENT #2: "I feel like we had a fight last week and I was still mad." 
 TEACHER: "So the real issue is something from before, and this spilled into recess. Here's the thing, though. Telling a group not to play with someone shuts them out of everything at once. That doesn’t seem fair. What can be done instead?" 
 STUDENT #2: "If I'm mad at her, I deal with her, I don't turn people against her." 
 TEACHER: "Anything to close it?" 
 STUDENT #2: "I shouldn't have said that to everyone. Sorry."

Group questions:
1. A student says they are only a 4 out of 10. What do you do?

## Scenario 4: PYP - "Boys should lead"  

Context : While picking a team leader, some students argue a boy should lead because "boys are better leaders."  

TEACHER: "Let's pause the vote for a second. I want to pick up on something. A few of you said a boy should lead because boys make better leaders. Let's actually think about that out loud. What makes someone a good team leader?" 
 STUDENT#1: "Someone organized and listens." 
 STUDENT #2: "Someone who keeps the group on track and is fair." 
 TEACHER: (writing them up) "Good. Organized, listens, fair, keeps people on track. Now here's my question. Which of those is something only a boy can do?" 
 STUDENT #1: (pause) "...None of them." 
 TEACHER: "Right. So when we say 'a boy should lead because boys are better,' we're skipping straight past all the things that actually make a leader and deciding based on gender instead. Is that fair to the girls who might have every one of those qualities?" 
 STUDENT #1: "No. Some of the best group organizers in here are girls." 
 TEACHER: "So here's the standard I want us to hold: leadership is about skills and qualities, not gender. That part's not up for debate. But how we pick, that's yours. How could you choose a leader based on those qualities we listed, instead of on who's a boy or a girl?" 
 STUDENT #2: "We could say what job we're doing and who's actually good at organizing that." 
 STUDENT #1: "Or nominate anyone and say what they'd be good at." 
 TEACHER: "That's a much stronger way to choose, and it's fair to everyone. Let's run it that way. Nominate someone and give a reason based on the qualities on the board." 

(Debrief: This scenario is a discussion to guide, not necessarily a conflict to mediate. Notice the mediator holds a firm boundary 'gender isn't a qualification' while letting students do the reasoning.) 

Group questions:
1. Students say a boy should lead. How do you pause the vote without shutting them down?
2. How do you hold the line that gender isn't a qualification while letting students do the reasoning?
3. What could students use instead to choose a leader?
 

## Scenario 5: MS/HS - "You're So Fat" 

Context: A student made a comment on another student’s body in the cafeteria.  

*Name the harm first: This is a body-shaming comment that made the student stop eating. 

TEACHER: (to the group in the moment) "Hang on. Commenting on someone's body or how much they eat isn't okay. That's the kind of thing that really sticks with a person, so we don't do it here." (Privately, to student #1) "Are you okay? On a 1–10, how ready are you to talk with them?" 
 STUDENT #1:(quiet) "I don't know. Maybe a 3. I just stopped being hungry." 
 TEACHER: "That tells me it hit hard, and that's exactly why it's worth naming. We can wait a bit if you want, or take it slow together. Your call." 
 STUDENT #1:"We can talk. Slow." 
 TEACHER: (both, to student #1) "Tell them what that was like." 
 STUDENT #1:"I felt embarrassed about eating. I just wanted more food and you made it feel like something's wrong with me." 
 TEACHER: (to student #2) "What did you hear?" 
 STUDENT #2: "That she got upset over a joke." 
 TEACHER: "No. It’s deeper than that. I heard her say it made her feel ashamed of eating, so she stopped. Say that back." 
 STUDENT #2: "You felt ashamed and you stopped eating because of what I said." 
 TEACHER: (to student #1) "Would you say that is accurate?" 
 STUDENT #1:"Yeah." 
 TEACHER: (to student #2) "Now you can explain your side." 
 STUDENT #2: "I thought it was a funny thing to say. I didn't think it'd actually get to her." 
 TEACHER: "Here's the part to remember. Comments about someone's body or eating can stick for a really long time. Longer than you'd guess. What can we do differently going forward?" 
 STUDENT #2: "I keep comments about people's bodies to myself." 
 TEACHER: "Anything to close it out?" 
 STUDENT #2: "I didn't mean to make you feel bad. I’m sorry. Let’s finish lunch." 

(Debrief note: the student stopped eating. The mediator honors the low readiness score and names why this kind of comment carries weight.) 

Group questions:
1. A student says they are a 3 out of 10 and stopped eating. What do you offer them?
2. The other student says it was just a joke. How do you show why it matters?
3. How do you name the harm to the group in the moment, before talking privately?


## Scenario 6: MS/HS - The Salute 

Context: Student does a 'heil hitler' salute in class. 

*This is not a two-sided conflict. Name the harm first, then teach. There is no "Student B" to mediate with. The target here is a whole community. 

TEACHER: (calm, firm, to the students) "Stop. Put your hands down. What you just did is called a Nazi salute. The people who use it today aren't just 'being edgy', they're sending a message to others that they agree with the Nazis The Nazis massacred millions of people for no reason other than they were Jewish, or gay, or disabled, or Polish, or Chinese, or Romani, and on and on." 
STUDENT #1: "I swear I didn't know what it meant." 
 TEACHER: "I believe that you might not have. That's exactly why we're going to talk about it." 
TEACHER: (to the group) "Here's what I need you to understand. Making that salute, or drawing a swastika, tells others that you think the Nazis were right. That it was a good thing to kill so many innocent people. 'It was just a joke' isn't something you get to say again. I don't think you actually believe people should be killed for being different, but that's the message you send when you do that salute." 
STUDENT #1: "We were just messing around." 
TEACHER: "I hear you, but this isn't something people see as 'just messing around'. Someone who doesn't know you and sees it will probably not make the same assumptions I did. Going forward, what do you do if you see a symbol or gesture and you don't know what it means?" 
 STUDENT #1: "Ask an adult instead of copying it." 
 TEACHER: "Exactly. I'm also going to make sure you learn a bit more about where this comes from, because this one matters." 

(Debrief: This scenario is about education + boundary, not reconciliation. Notice the mediator accepts "I didn't know" without letting it excuse the harm, and carries the teaching themselves.)  

Group questions:
1. A student says they didn't know what the gesture meant. What do you say?
2. How can we address behaviours that are unacceptible or reprehensible, but don't target specific students?
3. It can be awkward to address certain behaviours, like students making sexual noises. How have you approached these things?
 

## Scenario 7: MS/HS - Racism 

Context : A student assigned Japan for a research task makes a sweeping negative sterotype about the nation and its people. No Japanese students are present. 

TEACHER: "Let's stop there. You just said something about all Japanese people, and it wasn't kind. I want to unpack it, because it matters even though no one here is Japanese." 
STUDENT: "I mean, there's no Japanese kids here, so who cares?" 
 TEACHER: "That's actually the exact thing I want to touch on. Who we are is what we say when the person it's about isn't in the room. If it's not something you'd say to a Japanese classmate's face, that tells you something." 
 STUDENT: "It was just a joke about the country." 
 TEACHER: "You made a claim about a whole nation and millions of people. Is that a fact you could source, or a stereotype?" 
 STUDENT: "...A stereotype, I guess." 
 TEACHER: "Right. And in this class, part of your job is telling the difference. Two things going forward. One, stereotyping a whole country isn't okay here, present or not. Two, your task is to find out what's actually true about Japan and bring me three real things you learned. Sometimes the country you 'got stuck with' turns out to be the most interesting one." 

(Debrief: How do you address harm when there's no victim in the room to advocate for? Notice the mediator makes the absent group the point, and folds it into the academic task rather than turning it into pure discipline.) 

Group questions:
1. A student says it doesn't matter because no one from that country is in the room. What do you say?
2. How do you help a student tell a fact from a stereotype?
3. How can you fold this into the class task instead of treating it only as discipline?


## Scenario 8: MS/HS - Truth or Dare 

Context : A group is playing truth or dare. One student is asked "would you kiss [names another student]?" You intervene before they answer. The named student may or may not be present. 

TEACHER: "Stop. Hang on a sec." (Light, not alarmed. You're redirecting, not punishing.) "I heard the dare. I'm going to jump in before anyone answers." 
 STUDENT #1: "It's just truth or dare, we're not making trouble." 
 TEACHER: "The game's fine. I'm not shutting it down. Here's my one issue. The dare put the other person into it without them here to have any say. Answering it either way, yes or no, ends up being about their body and their feelings, and they didn't sign up for that." 
STUDENT #1: "We weren't being mean though." 
TEACHER: "I believe you weren't trying to. But think about it impacting them. If they heard it, they'd probably be really embarrassed. And if they didn't hear it, now there's a story going around about who'd kiss who and that always finds its way back to the person. Neither one's fair to them." 
 STUDENT #2: (relieved) "Yeah, I didn't really want to answer that." 
 TEACHER: "That's the other thing. You got put on the spot too. So, here's the easy fix. Keep playing, keep it about you all. Dares, silly stuff, questions about yourselves. Just leave other people who aren't in the game out of it. Deal?" 
 G: "Deal." 

(Debrief: This one "looks like fun," so the instinct is to walk past it, but that can still do harm. Notice the mediator preserves the game and gives a clean, usable rule: "keep it about the people playing.") 

Group questions:
1. The game looks harmless. When do you step in, and how do you keep your tone light?
2. How do you explain the harm to someone who isn't there?
3. What simple rule can students use to keep playing fairly?


## Scenario 9: MS/HS - "You're So Gay"

Context: One student calls another student “gay” meant as an insult in front of the class. 

*Name the harm before mediating. This uses an identity as an insult, which harms both the target and every LGBTQ+ student in earshot. 

TEACHER: (to the class, in the moment) "Let's pause. Using 'gay' as an insult isn't okay here. It tells people that who someone is, is something to laugh at. That's harmful, and it's not up for debate." (Then privately, to Student #1) "How ready are you to talk with them, 1–10? And you don't owe anyone an explanation around the insult." 
 STUDENT #1:"Maybe a 5." 
 TEACHER: (both, to student #1) "Go ahead." 
 STUDENT #1:"I felt humiliated. Everyone laughed, and it was like being 'gay' is the worst thing you could call someone. I don’t think it is" 
 TEACHER: (to student #2) "What did you hear?" 
 STUDENT #2: "That he got embarrassed." 
 TEACHER: "More than just embarrassed. I heard him say it made him feel like who a person is could be used as an insult. Say that back." 
 STUDENT #2: "You felt like I was making being gay a bad thing to be." 
 TEACHER: (to student #1) "Is that accurate?" 
 STUDENT #1:"Yeah." 
 TEACHER: (to student #2) "Your turn." 
 STUDENT #2: "I feel like everyone says it, I didn't really think about it." 
 TEACHER: "That's honest, and here's the piece I'll name. Even when a word gets tossed around a lot, using someone's identity as a put-down tells every kid in that room they'd be a punchline too. What can we do differently?" 
 STUDENT #2: "I stop using it like that." 
 TEACHER: "Anything to close it out?" 
 STUDENT #2: "That wasn't fair to you, and I'll cut it out." 

(Debrief : the harm reaches beyond the two students. The mediator addresses the room and repairs with the target.)

Group questions:
1. Student 2 says: "That he got embarrassed." What would you say next?
2. How do you address the whole class, not just the two students?
3. A student says everyone uses the word. What do you say?
